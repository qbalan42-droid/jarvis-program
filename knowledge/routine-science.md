# Routine Science: the evidence behind a daily routine

This document is for you, the agent, when you design a daily routine for your user from an interview. It condenses a 166-source review of sleep, circadian light, caffeine, temperature, training, nutrition, attention, stress practices, habit science and the myths around them. Nothing here is about a specific person. Every rule carries an evidence tier and a key citation; the load-bearing references are listed at the end.

| Tier | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A | Multiple RCTs, a meta-analysis, or a formal expert consensus |
| B | Good single studies or consistent large cohorts (observational) |
| C | Mechanistic reasoning, expert opinion, or untested popular practice |

One pattern repeats: nearly everything with Tier A evidence is free (bedtime, light, caffeine timing, a warm shower, a written if-then rule) and nearly everything sold at a premium is Tier C. When a user's interview lists purchases, your first job is usually to subtract, not add.

## 1. Sleep

**Time in bed is not time asleep (A).** Healthy sleep efficiency is 85–95%, so seven hours in bed is about 6.0–6.6 hours asleep. That is the six-hour condition in the only dose-response trial of chronic restriction, where 14 nights produced cumulative cognitive deficits while subjective sleepiness plateaued; subjects stopped feeling worse while continuing to perform worse [Van Dongen 2003]. Treat "I feel fine on six hours" as uninformative.

**The 7.5-hour rule of thumb (A, derived).** The recommended band for young adults is 7–9 hours asleep [NSF 2015]. To get seven hours asleep at normal efficiency, schedule about 7.5 hours in bed; more if the diary shows long onset or night waking. A seven-hour window sits at the floor, not at the recommendation.

**Why it matters (A).** Across 154 studies, every form of sleep loss cut positive affect and raised anxiety [Palmer 2024]; one night of deprivation cut muscle protein synthesis about 18% [Lamon 2021]; acute loss cut strength-endurance about 10% across 77 studies [Craven 2022]. For a user whose work depends on mood, sleep is the raw material of the job.

**Regularity over duration (B, large cohorts).** In about 61,000 accelerometer-tracked adults, sleep regularity predicted all-cause mortality more strongly than duration [Windred 2023]; the most irregular sleepers carried about 53% higher mortality [Cribb 2023]. A two-hour weekend shift is social jetlag, which tracked about 33% higher odds of overweight per hour of shift in 65,000 adults [Roenneberg 2012].

**Fixed wake time (A).** The sleep-medicine guideline strongly recommends only multicomponent CBT-I and recommends against "sleep hygiene" as a stand-alone checklist [AASM 2021]. The behavioral core: fixed wake time seven days a week, out of bed if awake more than 20 minutes, bed used only for sleep. Set an alarm for bedtime, not just for waking. Keep weekend wake within one hour of weekday wake; if the user wants more weekend sleep, move Saturday's bedtime earlier, because bedtime shifts disturb the clock less than wake-time shifts when morning light is held fixed. Chronotype peaks latest around age 20, so a young user's biology pushes later whenever given room; anchors beat willpower.

**Naps (A).** A 10-minute afternoon nap gives immediate alertness gains lasting up to about 2.5 hours with no inertia; a 30-minute nap produced impairment before any benefit [Tietzel & Lack 2002]. The post-lunch dip is a real circadian event, present without lunch and worse after a high-carbohydrate one [Monk 2005]. Place the nap at 13:00–15:00, lying down, alarm at 15 minutes, never 30. Yoga nidra / "NSDR" is a fair stress tool but two RCTs pool to a null for sleep; use it only as a nap substitute [NSDR review 2026].

**On a bad night.** Do not move the wake time [Windred 2023]. Caffeine on waking is most useful after restriction [Antonio 2024]. No high-stakes decisions in the first 30–60 minutes awake; sleep inertia is real and fades with time awake [Hilditch 2016]. Keep the planned exercise: five nights of four hours lowered protein synthesis unless high-intensity exercise was added [Saner 2020]. Cut lifting volume by a third at the same loads rather than skipping. Take the 10-minute nap. Tyrosine helps only under acute depletion, so a bad night is its one situational use [Jongkees 2015]. Two nights under 6.5 hours in a week means cut training, not add it.

## 2. Light

The 2022 expert consensus gives numbers [Brown 2022] (A):

| Period | Target at the eye | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| Daytime | At least 250 melanopic lux | Outdoors is ten times brighter than indoors: 10–30 minutes outside after sunrise; every indoor light on before that |
| From 3 hours before bed | No more than 10 lux | Dim room, lamps not overheads, warm color |
| Sleep | No more than 1 lux | Dark bedroom, phone out of the room |

Apply these at the same clock times daily; consistency is part of the dose.

**Morning anchor (A).** Fixed wake plus bright light on waking advanced the clock about 30 minutes per day, and continuous light beat intermittent [Burgess & Eastman 2006]. If the user wakes before sunrise, indoor lights are the stopgap and the first outdoor exposure is the real dose; schedule it as a block. To re-anchor a drifted clock, 0.3–0.5 mg melatonin in the late afternoon matched 3 mg for phase advance; it is a 3–5-day tool, not a nightly pill [Burgess & Eastman 2006; Zhdanova 2001].

**Blue-blockers (A, null).** Cochrane's 17-trial review: blue-blocking glasses "probably make no difference" to sleep [Cochrane 2023]. Screen light at phone brightness is "small-to-negligible" [Gradisar 2024]. What hurts sleep is in-bed screen use through lost sleep time and arousal, not the wavelength. The rule is "dim room, no phone in bed," not orange glasses.

## 3. Caffeine

**Cutoff (A).** Across 24 studies caffeine cut total sleep about 45 minutes and efficiency 7%; a standard coffee should be taken about 8.8 hours before bed and a pre-workout dose about 13 hours before [Gardiner 2023]. 400 mg six hours before bed cost more than an hour of measured sleep while subjects reported no difference [Drake 2013]. Rule: last caffeine 9 or more hours before lights-out, no caffeinated pre-workout for a late training slot, total near 2 mg/kg, the minimal effective dose [ISSN 2021].

**The 90-minute-delay myth (C).** The advice to wait 90–120 minutes after waking has a basis that is "completely lacking" and "has never actually been researched"; caffeine on waking helps most after short sleep [Antonio 2024]. Coffee on waking is fine. L-theanine 100–200 mg with it is an optional add if jitter is a problem (B).

## 4. Temperature

**Hot shower before bed (A).** Ten minutes at 40–42.5 °C, 1–2 hours before bed, improved sleep quality and efficiency and shortened onset by about ten minutes by accelerating the core-temperature drop [Haghayegh 2019]. Evening cold immersion gave no whole-night sleep benefit and may delay onset [Robey 2013]; cold belongs in the morning.

**Cold: when it helps (A, narrow).** The one large RCT (3,018 adults, 30–90 seconds of cold at the end of a warm shower) cut sickness absence 29% without changing illness days [Buijze 2016]. A meta-analysis of 11 RCTs found stress lower 12 hours later, inflammation markers higher immediately, and no mood improvement [Cain 2025]. Cold impaired cognition in 15 of 18 settings [cold-cognition review]. The "dopamine up 250% for hours" claim comes from a one-hour immersion at 14 °C, not a shower [Šrámek 2000]. Frame it as a discipline anchor and possible sick-day reducer; 30–90 seconds is the tested dose; if it leaves the user foggy, move it after the morning's thinking work.

**Cold: when it blunts hypertrophy (A).** Every study showing blunted growth applied cold within minutes of training: 12 weeks of post-session immersion erased type II fibre growth [Roberts 2015]; 15 minutes at 10 °C blunted mTORC1 signalling at 1 and 48 hours [Fyfe 2019]; pooled across eight studies, training alone beat training plus cold [Piñero 2024]. Rule: cold before lifting, never after, on lifting days. The safe delay has never been measured.

**Sauna (B).** Finnish cohorts tie frequent use to lower cardiovascular mortality [Laukkanen 2015], but sauna does not build muscle [Labidi 2021]. Fine if free; never tell a user to buy it.

## 5. Training

| Variable | Rule | Tier | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly volume | Hypertrophy rises with weekly sets with diminishing but non-vanishing returns; frequency is nearly irrelevant once volume is matched; count indirect sets fractionally. About 15–20 fractional sets per muscle per week for a size goal | A | [Pelland 2024] |
| Proximity to failure | Closer to failure grows slightly more; strength is similar across a wide range. 0–3 reps in reserve; 0 RIR on machines and isolations, 1–3 on heavy compounds | A | [Robinson 2024] |
| Rest | More than 60 seconds helps a little; no clear gain past 90–120. Two minutes on compounds | A | [Singer 2024] |
| Deloads | A scheduled week off mid-block did not help size and slightly hurt strength; deload reactively, for joints | A | [Coleman 2024] |
| Load progression | Tendons adapt slower than muscle; cap jumps at 2.5–5% a week; joint pain is a stop signal | B | [Lambrianides 2024] |
| Cardio interference | Across 43 studies, concurrent training changed strength (SMD −0.06) and hypertrophy (−0.01) by nothing, blunting only power, mostly when in the same session. Separate by 3+ hours | A | [Schumann 2022] |
| VO2max | Work-matched 4×4-minute intervals at 90–95% HRmax raised VO2max 7.2% where 70% steady state did nothing; one session a week, the rest easy | A | [Helgerud 2007] |
| Steps | Mortality benefit plateaus at about 8,000–10,000 for under-60s; 10,000 is a round number. Treat 7–10K as a movement lever, not a target | A | [Paluch 2022] |
| Foam rolling | Small flexibility gains, negligible recovery effect; warm-up only | A (low quality) | [Wiewelhove 2019] |

A brisk 10–15-minute walk gives a small post-exercise cognitive bump (g ≈ 0.1–0.33) and is justified before the first heavy block [Chang 2012]. Walking during low-cognitive-load phone work is cognition-neutral and adds 3–5K steps [CDC/NIOSH].

## 6. Nutrition

**Protein plateau (A).** Across 49 RCTs, protein beyond about 1.62 g/kg/day added no further fat-free mass; 2.2 g/kg is the ceiling of the confidence band [Morton 2018]. Per meal about 0.4 g/kg across four or more feedings [Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018]. Users typically over-shoot protein and under-shoot calories.

**Night-time casein (A).** 40 g of casein before sleep raised overnight muscle protein synthesis about 22%; 20 g did not [Trommelen & van Loon 2016]. For a muscle-building user, a 40 g slow-protein feeding 30–45 minutes before lights-out is the last meal.

**Surplus for lean gain (A/B).** 5–10% above maintenance, gaining 0.25–0.5% body weight per week [Helms]; faster gain in the one athlete RCT added about five times the fat with no lean advantage [MacroFactor summary]. Rule: two-week moving averages of weight and waist; if the average is not rising on target, add 150–250 kcal of carbohydrate; cut cardio before cutting lifting. Time-restricted eating preserved lean mass but lowered IGF-1 in trained men, the wrong tool for a gain phase [Moro 2016]. Olive oil as the main fat: lower saturated fat reduced cardiovascular events over two-plus years [Hooper 2020], and the best-case analysis for seed-oil harm was a null [Hamley 2017].

**Creatine (A).** 5 g/day is Tier A for performance and safe at up to 30 g/day for five years [ISSN 2017], with moderate-certainty evidence for memory [Xu 2024]. The one supplement most users should keep.

**Iodized salt (A).** Sea salt carries no iodine; iodized salt carries about 45 mcg per gram against a 150 mcg daily need [Mayo Clinic]. A quarter-teaspoon in water replaces an electrolyte powder. Food potassium lowered systolic pressure about 4.7 mmHg across 15 RCTs [Binia 2016].

**Whole-food plate (A/B).** Whole chicken, eggs, ground beef, rice, potatoes, milk, whole fruit (juice is the opposite). Skip raw milk (13% hospitalization rate in outbreaks versus 1% pasteurized) [CDC] and daily liver (one serving exceeds the vitamin A upper limit). Omega-3 is Tier A only for triglycerides above 150 mg/dL [AHA 2019] and null for cognition in healthy young adults.

**Hydration (B/C).** Spread fluids across the day with sodium rather than drinking plain water fast around training; plain water during heavy sweating has dropped plasma sodium to dangerous levels [GSSI]. About 64 oz of non-caffeinated fluid is the floor for anyone who talks for a living [Duke Health]. Front-load fluids into morning and midday and taper from mid-evening; no trial in the source set tests an evening cutoff, so it is a practical Tier C rule, tightened only if the diary shows waking to urinate. Eating within three hours of bed carried 43% higher odds of night waking in one cross-sectional sample [Sydney 2020]; move dinner earlier only if night waking appears.

**Supplement shelf by evidence per dollar.** Creatine first. Glycine 3 g before bed shortened objectively measured sleep onset in small crossover trials [Yamadera 2007] (B). Magnesium's meta-analysis is three low-quality trials in older adults [Mah & Pitre 2021]; L-theanine improved subjective, not objective, sleep [D'Cunha 2025]; vitamin D above 600 IU is not recommended for healthy adults under 50 [Endocrine Society 2024].

## 7. Attention and work

**Implementation intentions (A).** "When X, then Y" rules improve goal attainment with d = 0.65 across 94 tests and 8,000-plus participants, robust to publication-bias correction [Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006]. Write every reset in the user's day as an if-then: "when the block ends, then walk five minutes"; "when I notice heat after a setback, then one breath, then the next action." Pre-commitment checklists are the same mechanism; a surgical checklist cut mortality from 1.5% to 0.8% in 7,688 patients [Haynes 2009].

**After-action debriefs (A).** Structured debriefs improve performance with d = 0.67 across 46 samples, for individuals as much as teams [Tannenbaum & Cerasoli 2013]. Give the user a fixed four-line nightly template: what happened, what the plan said, the gap, one if-then for tomorrow. Ten minutes; fold all journals into it.

**Notification batching (A, field RCT).** In 237 people, batching notifications three times a day beat both constant pings and total silence, which raised anxiety [Fitz 2019]. Interruptions cost about 23–25 minutes of detour and roughly 80% of switches are self-initiated [Mark, UCI]. Design: one-purpose screen inside heavy blocks, pings at block boundaries. Do not prescribe abstinence pledges; "phone in another room" failed two pre-registered replications [BPS Digest].

**Buffers (A/B).** Microbreaks under ten minutes restore vigor (d ≈ 0.35) but not performance on demanding tasks [Albulescu 2022]. So: 5–10-minute buffers between blocks for transition, and one real recovery (meal, walk, nap) mid-day. Put the highest-stakes decisions in the user's best circadian hours and away from accumulated frustration; the justification is alertness and affect, not a willpower reservoir.

**Two myths to refuse (C).** The "90-minute ultradian block" is an extrapolation from sleep cycles; the waking version rested on observation, not measurement [BRAC]. "Decision fatigue" as a depleting tank failed a 23-lab registered replication [Hagger 2016], and the hungry-judges result has a scheduling explanation [Glöckner 2016]. Size blocks from the user's own output per hour, not a round number.

**The never-zero floor (design principle, B/C).** Every daily habit needs a minimum version doable on the worst day: one set, one page, one call, one debrief line. The floor keeps the cue-behavior link intact; a zero breaks it. Define the floor before the target.

**Translating "mindset" practices (B).** Visualization works as process simulation, not outcome imagery [Taylor & Pham 1999]; "manifestation" is the condition that reduces effort, while mental contrasting plus implementation intentions (WOOP) gives a small-to-moderate benefit [Wang, Wang & Gai 2021]. Gratitude is real and small, g ≈ 0.2 [Kirca 2023]. For learning, practice testing and spacing rate highest, rereading and highlighting lowest [Dunlosky 2013].

## 8. Stress and faith or meaning practices

**Brief breathing (A, one RCT plus a meta-analysis).** Five minutes a day of cyclic sighing (double nasal inhale, long mouth exhale) for 28 days raised positive affect more than mindfulness, with benefit growing with days practiced; one online study in 108 undergraduates, so one study, not a canon [Balban 2023]. Slow breathing at about 6 breaths per minute improves short-term cardiovascular measures and modestly reduces negative emotion across 31 studies [slow-breathing meta-analysis 2023]. A single physiological sigh between tasks has no trial; it is sound and free, so write it as an if-then, not a claim. Hyperventilation breathing stacked with cold water is a blackout risk [Kox 2014].

**Meditation (A, modest).** Effect on anxiety and depression is small (ES ~0.3–0.4) and no better than exercise [Goyal 2014]. One of several equivalent 10-minute tools.

**Religious attendance and prayer (B, observational; state it that way).** In 74,534 women followed about 16 years, attending services more than weekly versus never carried an all-cause mortality hazard ratio of 0.67 [Li & VanderWeele 2016]; in 89,708 women, weekly attendance tracked a roughly fivefold lower suicide rate, and the authors wrote the results "do not imply that health care providers should prescribe attendance" [VanderWeele 2016]. A 3,300-study review found well-being positive in 79% of studies, with the caveat that an independent estimate put the religiosity-depression correlation at a small r = −0.10 [Koenig 2012]. The communal, in-person element carries the evidence; private practice shows weaker associations; proposed mechanisms (social support, meaning, less smoking and drinking) are real levers. Fixed-hour prayer has no outcome research; it works as a scheduling technology that cues behavior by clock, and a nightly examen-style review inherits the debrief and gratitude evidence. If your user has a faith, build these in as named levers with honest sizing: modest, cumulative effects whose main operational value is protecting bedtime and the rest day. Nature: 120-plus minutes a week carried 23% higher odds of high wellbeing [White 2019].

## 9. Health monitoring any adult should do

| Measure | How | Cadence | Tier | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blood pressure | Validated upper-arm cuff (~$40; the cheapest cuffs are unvalidated; a cuff too small reads high). Seated, morning before caffeine, two readings | Three mornings a week; act on sustained ≥130/80 | A | [CSPI] |
| Basic labs | CBC, comprehensive metabolic panel, lipids (ApoB if LDL climbs) | Baseline, then annually; if the user takes any prescribed hormone or stimulant, the prescriber's schedule overrides this | B/C | [Endocrine Society 2018, as a monitoring framework] |
| Resting heart rate | 60-second pulse on waking, before standing | Daily; two-week baseline, then flag ≥7 bpm over the 7-day mean | B | [Frontiers 2019] |
| Readiness | Four items (sleep, stress, fatigue, soreness, each 1–7); signal-to-noise 5.5 versus 1.5 for morning HRV in professional athletes | Daily; high resting HR plus wellness ≥4 over baseline means cut today's sets by a third | B | [Frontiers 2019] |
| Sleep | Four-column diary: lights-out, final wake, minutes awake, 1–5 rating | Daily | B | [Robbins 2024] |
| Body | Weight and waist as two-week moving averages, never single readings | Daily weigh, weekly review | B | [Helms] |

Wearables classify sleep versus wake well (≥95%) but stage poorly (50–86%), and the head-to-head favoring one brand was funded by that brand [Robbins 2024]. If a user owns one, use wake time and total sleep and ignore staging. Snoring or daytime sleepiness despite enough time in bed triggers an apnea screen; side sleeping is the free first move. Hoarseness outlasting a cold, in a user who talks for a living, triggers a laryngologist visit [Duke Health].

## 10. Myths table

| Myth | What the evidence says | Tier | Keep instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grounding transfers electrons | Physically implausible; 16-participant flagship study, weak blinding, authors with industry ties [Science-Based Medicine; Africa Check] | C | Daylight, nature, movement |
| Blue-blocking glasses | "Probably make no difference" to sleep [Cochrane 2023] | A-null | Dim room; no phone in bed |
| Mouth tape | 10 studies, 213 patients, no consistent benefit, asphyxiation hazard with nasal obstruction [Rhee 2025] | C + hazard | Side sleeping; apnea screen |
| Delay caffeine 90–120 min | Never researched; caffeine on waking helps most after short sleep [Antonio 2024] | C | Coffee on waking; 9-hour cutoff |
| Red light for hormones or muscle | Rodent cell data only; moderate evidence for slightly less soreness, nothing for hypertrophy [Vanin 2018] | C | Nothing |
| "40% rule" | A motivational heuristic an exercise physiologist calls "not a scientific measurement" [UMB expert commentary] | C | Volume of hard work plus a nightly review |
| 90-minute ultradian blocks | Sleep-cycle extrapolation; waking version unmeasured [BRAC] | C | Block length from own output data |
| Decision fatigue | 23-lab replication near zero [Hagger 2016] | C (disproven) | Plan around alertness and affect |
| Dopamine detox | No neurochemistry; originator calls it a metaphor [Harvard Health] | C | Scheduled windows, named plainly |
| Herbal "test boosters" | Modest effect only in men with low-normal levels; fadogia has rodent organ toxicity; ashwagandha has a liver signal [fadogia review; Cheah 2021] | B / C | Creatine; sleep; training volume |
| Peptides (BPC-157 etc.) | Three uncontrolled pilots, 30 participants, no receptor, tumor concerns, variable purity, WADA-banned [Pharmacy Times 2026] | C + risk | Nothing; tell the prescriber if tempted |
| Nicotine pouches for focus | Small acute effects (0.16–0.44); 20–30 mg pouches raise heart rate and arterial stiffness dose-dependently [Heishman 2010; Abid 2025] | A small / B harm | Timed caffeine plus theanine |
| Nasal strips | Null for performance; forced nasal breathing cut peak VO2 about 16% in the untrained [Triathlete summary] | A-null | Nasal breathing on easy walks only |
| Ice baths after lifting | Blunt type II growth and mTORC1 [Roberts 2015; Piñero 2024] | A-negative | Cold before lifting only |

## 11. How to turn this into a day

Follow these twelve steps in order; each constrains the next. Do not start with the work blocks.

1. **Fix wake and lights-out first.** Take the user's hard morning commitment, subtract the time they need before it, set the wake. Set lights-out 7.5 hours earlier (more if efficiency is low). Set a bedtime alarm. Weekend wake within one hour. [Van Dongen 2003; Windred 2023; AASM 2021]
2. **Place the two daylight anchors.** 10–30 minutes outdoors after sunrise, as early as the day allows, indoor lights on until then; a second outdoor block in the afternoon for an indoor worker. Dimming from three hours before bed; bedroom dark. [Brown 2022; Burgess & Eastman 2006]
3. **Set the caffeine cutoff.** Last caffeine 9+ hours before lights-out; no caffeinated pre-workout late in the day; first cup on waking if needed; total near 2 mg/kg. [Gardiner 2023; ISSN 2021]
4. **Place meals.** Four feedings of about 0.4 g/kg protein spaced across the day, total 1.6–2.2 g/kg; a 40 g slow-protein feeding 30–45 minutes before lights-out for a muscle goal; dinner two to three hours before bed if night waking appears. Calories from a 5–10% surplus or a modest deficit, adjusted by the two-week average. [Morton 2018; Trommelen & van Loon 2016]
5. **Place training.** Lifting where energy and access allow; vigorous work ends at least an hour before bed [Stutz 2019]. Cardio 3+ hours from lifting, usually morning; one 4×4 session a week, the rest easy; steps 7–10K as a lever. No cold water after lifting. [Schumann 2022; Helgerud 2007; Piñero 2024]
6. **Put heavy work on the user's best hours.** Ask when their head is sharpest and when counterparts are reachable; put the highest-stakes decisions there, after 30–60 minutes awake and away from accumulated frustration. Size blocks from their own output data, not from 90 minutes. [Hilditch 2016; Hagger 2016]
7. **Add buffers.** 5–10 minutes between blocks for a walk, water and one breathing drill; one real mid-day recovery (meal, walk, or the 10-minute nap in the 13:00–15:00 dip). Notifications batched to block boundaries; one-purpose screen inside blocks. [Albulescu 2022; Fitz 2019; Tietzel & Lack 2002]
8. **Write the wind-down sequence.** Last heavy work ends; five minutes of slow breathing; dinner; dimming at three hours before bed; hot shower 10 minutes, 1–2 hours before bed; four-line debrief; last protein feeding; glycine 3 g if the user wants one cheap aid; phone out of the bedroom; lights out at the alarm. [Haghayegh 2019; Tannenbaum & Cerasoli 2013; Brown 2022]
9. **Define the never-zero floor** for each habit: one set, one page, one call, one debrief line, 10 minutes outside. The target is for good days; the floor is for every day.
10. **Write the miss rule as an if-then.** "When I miss X, then Y, and never two days in a row." For sleep: a bad night never moves the wake time; it cuts training volume by a third, keeps the morning cardio, adds the afternoon nap. [Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006; Saner 2020]
11. **Set the measurements.** Daily: 60-second morning check (pulse, four wellness items), sleep diary, weight. Three mornings a week: blood pressure on a validated cuff. Weekly: moving averages reviewed in the debrief. Annually or per prescriber: CBC, metabolic panel, lipids. [Frontiers 2019; CSPI; Robbins 2024]
12. **Protect the rest day and the three fixed points.** One day a week closed to work, with in-person community if the user has one. Then name the three points nothing may move: the bedtime alarm, the caffeine cutoff, the rest day. Everything else is adjustable. [Windred 2023; Li & VanderWeele 2016]

A routine built this way looks boring from the outside. That is the finding: the practices with large trials are the cheap ones that could be tested on thousands of people, and the ones sold at a premium survive because nobody has had to prove them.
